In the past couple years, it feels like everyone has shared their “equally important” opinion on AI in software development, whether they are a developer or not. I am no different, and I am not trying to convince you that my opinion is more important or more correct. In fact, I encourage you to challenge me and tell me where I’m wrong. However, I wanted to write about it from my point of view, as I have learned a lot in this field the past couple of years.

There are certain facts about software in AI that we have found: it has made code development faster, and the barrier for learning to code is lower. These two facts combined have led many, including pioneers in the industry in the early days of AI, to predict that software engineers will no longer be a thing in the future. I strongly disagree with that, and some of them have since softened on that stance. The job description has simply shifted.

Some people say coding is dead. They are right, in a sense. I haven’t manually coded a line in forever. In fact, that skill has probably atrophied. My time is better spent on making decisions: what problems am I trying to solve, who am I helping, how should we design the solution, should this solution only apply to this problem and remain simple, or can we scale it to address other similar issues? That’s what an engineer is for: solving problems, and finding problems to solve. Coding used to be a big part of that, but now we don’t have to spend as much time on it.

Therefore, my workday consists of many conversations with an LLM, mainly brainstorming, then letting it run and make the code. After that, depending on how much confidence you have in the output and how much you feel you need to understand its implementation, you can review and change the code (which I do at work) or just let it rip, push and run to production (which I do for personal projects). 

Letting it rip is something that people are doing more and more, and it can produce a lot of “AI slop” code. And for some projects, that’s true, and I don’t care. Especially if it’s one that nobody uses or nothing depends on it. But for some other projects that use AI for development, and I hear people complain about AI slop, I would pose this question: was it really better without AI? I can tell you when I was first starting out at programming, I was horrible at it, and that code ran on production servers. Years later, I still look back on some code that I wrote, with and without AI, and wonder why on earth I made some of those decisions. I have a feeling that will continue for the rest of my life. I would also point out, AI is finding so many bugs and vulnerabilities that were introduced years ago into systems, before AI was a thing. It is simply a tool. A very powerful one, which could be compared to a monkey with a machine gun depending on who is creating what, but a tool nonetheless. 

Now, I’ve talked a little on how AI has changed how we code, but it has also revolutionized what we code. We can make so many new and exciting things.

The first and big thing that people wanted to do with AI was make chatbots. Make all the chatbots, everywhere, to answer every question a person could ever have. And there is a lot of use there. AI is extremely good at research, and I hardly ever find myself googling something unless its to fact check something AI recommended. You could make a chatbot over some internal company knowledge so people don’t have to go asking around for information when they have a question. That is a useful product to have. 

But there is so much more we could do. We can now automate complex judgement decisions.

For example, if you asked me a couple years ago to create a program to summarize an article or a big mess of text, I would tell you that would be a complex and brittle program. People made ways to do it, but it couldn’t really rewrite information into natural sounding languages as well as you would want. Nowadays we can do something like find an article, feed it to an LLM with a prompt like “you are a cybersecurity expert, summarize this incident for me” and it would do a perfectly fine job. That might seem small, but put it in the context of an automation and scale it, there are so many possibilities.

Imagine a system where you get a ticket saying that a server could be compromised by running a couple commands. You could design something that would read that ticket, run those commands, decide whether or not the finding is correct, and either close the ticket as a false positive or reach out to the owner to remediate that finding. Now, the design, capabilities, and tuning of that system will be complex depending on your needs (which is where software engineering comes in), but it can be done. This is one pipeline that would sit in the cybersecurity world, but you can apply this to other businesses. 

Customer support is a lot of the same stuff over and over again. Sometimes there are edge cases that require a human, but most times a customer needs help because they are getting an error. Why not have a system that checks the error, finds what caused it, decides what needs to be done, implements an approved solution, and asks if the customer is satisfied? It would be a complex system, but one that would save a lot of boring, repeatable work, and would hopefully make the customer experience much faster and easier. 

This technology is, rightfully so, taking the world by storm. It has also become a heavily used (and misused) buzzword, but that’s a separate conversation. It’s fascinating to watch it develop and I enjoy finding different ways to use it in my job and in my life. These opinions have come through a couple of years working with AI, but who knows what will happen or how it will be used in a decade or two. It is still so new, there are so many theories, and it will be so interesting to see which ones play out. Except for the one where it ends humanity, hopefully that doesn’t happen.
